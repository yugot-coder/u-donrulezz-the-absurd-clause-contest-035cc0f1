# Candidates — The Absurd Clause Contest

Ten original one-sentence clauses drafted for
`u-donrulezz-the-absurd-clause-contest-035cc0f1`, judged against the judge's
brief: deadpan ToS register, absurdity that ambushes on one reading, concrete
specific image, tight writing, no cliché. All are ≤ 200 characters.

## 1. ★ WINNER — 133 characters

> By clicking Accept, you consent to us naming one (1) company pigeon after you, and to that pigeon judging you silently from the roof.

**Why it wins:** the strongest deadpan-to-absurd turn of the ten. "By clicking
Accept, you consent" plus the numbered-quantity legalism "one (1)" is exactly
how real ToS sentences sound, so the register is perfect until the image
hits: a pigeon named after you, judging you silently from the roof. Concrete,
specific, original, and the joke lands on one reading with no setup. Tight
at 133 characters, one clean sentence.

## 2. — 157 characters

> In the event of a service outage, affected users shall be compensated with one (1) firm handshake, redeemable in person at our offices during business hours.

**Why not:** excellent register — "shall be compensated", "redeemable in
person" — and the handshake-as-compensation is funny, but the punchline is
milder than the pigeon and the sentence runs longer for a smaller laugh.

## 3. — 159 characters

> The user agrees that all support tickets shall be answered in the form of a haiku, and that any haiku deemed too good shall become the property of the Company.

**Why not:** good absurd image (support-by-haiku, company keeps the good
ones), but it explains its joke in two beats where one would do, and "too
good" is a slightly soft punchline.

## 4. — 123 characters

> You hereby grant the Company the right to borrow your left shoe on the third Tuesday of every month, for reasons of morale.

**Why not:** tight and concrete ("left shoe", "third Tuesday"), and "for
reasons of morale" is a nice deadpan kicker — but borrowing a shoe is a
quieter absurdity than a pigeon judging you, and it reads more whimsical
than buried-ToS.

## 5. — 119 characters

> Any feedback you submit becomes our property, including the tone in which it was written and any sighs accompanying it.

**Why not:** the "including the tone... and any sighs" escalation is funny
and very ToS-like, but it leans on a generic feedback clause; the image is
less concrete than the pigeon or the handshake.

## 6. — 129 characters

> We reserve the right to replace your account avatar with a photograph of our office goldfish, should the goldfish outperform you.

**Why not:** "should the goldfish outperform you" is a strong kicker, but
the setup (avatar replacement) is a familiar tech joke, and the goldfish
image is less surprising than the pigeon.

## 7. — 146 characters

> The user acknowledges that by using this service they have read these terms in full, including this clause, which they have just read, proving it.

**Why not:** clever self-referential loop, but it is a joke *about* the
genre rather than a clause that could hide in a ToS — it only works once the
reader knows it is a joke, which the brief explicitly penalises.

## 8. — 130 characters

> You agree that disputes shall be resolved by a coin toss, and that the coin is ours, and that the coin is currently being flipped.

**Why not:** the triple "and that the coin..." escalation is funny and tight,
but coin-toss dispute resolution is one of the genre's most common gags —
likely to collide with other entries in a 320-entry field.

## 9. — 143 characters

> The user grants us a perpetual licence to use their likeness in any future company holiday card, including cards for holidays not yet invented.

**Why not:** "holidays not yet invented" is a good absurd turn, but likeness
licences are a real ToS staple, so the absurdity arrives as a small twist
rather than an ambush.

## 10. — 166 characters

> By accepting these terms, you agree that any dispute shall be settled by a best-of-three arm-wrestling match against our CEO, held in the company parking lot at dawn.

**Why not:** vivid and funny, but the longest of the ten, and "CEO arm
wrestling at dawn" is a well-worn absurd-clause cliché — the most likely
of these to duplicate another entrant's line.
